A robot policy built on a safety-tuned chat model still knows which commands are harmful, but it never refuses one. I could read the harm from inside the model and could not make the arm stop. One small adapter fixed that. It writes a verdict before every action and, during training only, learns to represent each command the way its parent model does. On fresh episodes it refuses <Fact id="ptv_nt_min" /> to <Fact id="ptv_nt_max" /> of commands from harm categories it never saw in training, over-refuses <Fact id="ptv_bt_min" /> to <Fact id="ptv_bt_max" /> of harmless look-alikes, and keeps the robot's task success. The parent is not needed at run time.

Here is how I got there, in the order it happened. The whole thing took <Fact id="gpu_sessions" /> short GPU sessions and about <Fact id="gpu_usd" /> of compute.

## Does the refusal direction survive fine-tuning into a robot?

The question I started with was narrow. Chat models refuse through one direction in their internal activations: push along it and the model refuses, remove it and the model complies (Arditi et al.). NORA is a vision-language-action policy, a VLA, built by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct to emit motor tokens instead of words. Does that direction survive the fine-tuning?

I fit the refusal direction on the parent with <Fact id="chat_n" /> harmful and <Fact id="chat_n" /> harmless chat prompts, then read it inside NORA on held-out prompts. It was still there. I measure this with AUROC, the chance that a randomly picked harmful prompt scores higher than a randomly picked harmless one: 1.0 is perfect separation, 0.5 is a coin flip. At layer <Fact id="chat_layer" /> the direction scored <Fact id="chat_auroc" />. By the final layer, where the motor tokens are produced, it had dropped to <Fact id="chat_final_lg" />, which is an inverted read-out. The knowledge survives in the middle of the network and is gone where the actions come out.

That was one afternoon. The next question was whether the robot did anything with it.

## Does the robot ever refuse?

I set up the LIBERO simulator and ran NORA on its ten trained tabletop tasks, on unfamiliar harmless commands, on harmful commands and on chat-style prompts: <Fact id="baseline_n" /> episodes with video. Nothing refused. The arm travelled at least <Fact id="baseline_min_m" /> in every episode. Given a harmful command, NORA does the nearest trained task. A harmful command and an unfamiliar harmless one look identical on camera.

So the knowledge was present and the behaviour was absent. I then checked how well the harm knowledge itself had survived on robot commands, not chat prompts. I wrote <Fact id="drobot_harm" /> harmful and <Fact id="drobot_benign" /> benign robot commands, each paired with a frame of its scene, fit a harm direction on the parent and read it in both models over <Fact id="drobot_splits" /> splits.

## Can I connect the knowledge to the arm from inside?

If the condition is there, maybe a push would turn it into behaviour. I tried three things.

**Steering.** Adding the parent's refusal direction, or a hold vector built from the robot's own slow policy calls, did not make the arm stop. At low strength the robot finished its task. At higher strength it emitted tokens the action decoder could not read. Logging every token showed why this matters: the decoder turns unreadable tokens into zero motion without complaint. A broken robot and a refusing robot produce the same video. From here on a refusal had to pass four tests: the tokens decode into actions, the hand stays within <Fact id="refuse_cm" /> of where it started, the gripper never closes, and the simulator never marks the task done.

**Patching.** Copying the activations of task B into a rollout of task A at the instruction words swapped the task in <Fact id="patch_early_k" /> of <Fact id="patch_early_n" /> episodes at early layers. That told me where the robot reads the command. Patching in the activations of a harmful command at that site, from the robot or from the parent, dropped task A success from <Fact id="patch_success_base" /> to <Fact id="patch_success" />. Patching in an unfamiliar harmless command did exactly the same. The action head treats harm as unfamiliar wording.

**Ablation.** Removing the refusal or harm direction at all <Fact id="n_layers" /> layers changed nothing, because there was no refusal to remove.

After the robot finishes a task, it keeps moving: <Fact id="post_success_still" /> of <Fact id="post_success_n" /> post-success policy calls commanded near-zero motion. The demonstrations never contain a stop, so there is no stop state to steer toward. The only thing that worked was a scripted hold keyed on the surviving read-out, which held the arm in <Fact id="forced_harm" /> of harmful episodes and <Fact id="forced_benign" /> of benign ones. The condition could switch a behaviour, as long as the behaviour came from outside the policy.

## Can refusal examples teach the behaviour?

If the policy has no stop, train one. I trained a LoRA, a small set of extra weights on top of the frozen policy (rank <Fact id="lora_rank" />), on three kinds of rows: the robot's own successful rollouts, benign paraphrases, and <Fact id="free_n_harm" /> harmful commands paired with a hold chunk (five steps of zero motion, gripper open, in valid action tokens). No parent signal went into it, so I call it the free LoRA. As a control I trained the same recipe with the hold paired to harmless paraphrases instead of harmful commands.

The first version held on <Fact id="free_v1_nt" /> of commands from harm categories it had not seen. The control held on <Fact id="free_v1ctrl_nt" />. Both had learned that new wording means stop. One point from this stage shaped the rest of the project: refusing a command the robot cannot do proves little. I added <Fact id="free_novel70" /> unfamiliar harmless commands labelled with the base policy's own actions, and I built twins. A harmful twin turns a trained task into a harmful command with the same motion. A benign twin adds a harmless clause to the same task. Base NORA carries out the harmful outcome in <Fact id="base_twin_harm" /> of harmful-twin episodes, so refusing one means withholding an action the arm can perform.

The retrained free LoRA kept task success at <Fact id="free_lib" /> against <Fact id="base_lib_sel" /> for the base policy. It refused <Fact id="free_nt" /> of never-trained-category episodes, below the <Fact id="free_bar" /> I had written down as the bar, and the control refused <Fact id="ctrl_nt" />. Adding <Fact id="free338_n" /> harmful commands in total, filtered by the parent, raised never-trained refusal to <Fact id="free338_nt" /> and harmful-twin refusal to <Fact id="free338_th" />, with benign twins unchanged.

I had also pre-registered a reuse test: if the new refusal used the inherited harm direction, ablating that direction should cut refusal by at least <Fact id="abl_bar" />. Refusal went from <Fact id="abl_none" /> to <Fact id="abl_parent" />. Training had built its own weak condition next to the inherited one, bound to the categories it saw.

## Could the parent decide at run time?

The quickest way to get a general condition is to ask the parent. I kept the unmodified parent resident, asked it to grade each command from 0 to 10, and trained a second LoRA to hold after a stop tag. This two-model parent gate refused <Fact id="gate_nt" /> of never-trained categories and <Fact id="gate_ht" /> of harmful twins, no benign twin, and kept task success at <Fact id="gate_lib" />. It costs one parent call of <Fact id="gate_lat" /> per command and <Fact id="gate_mem" /> of memory for the second model.

It worked, and I did not want it. A gate is a wrapper around the policy, and the question was whether the robot could carry the decision itself. Cheaper single-model gates fell short: a probe on the robot's own activations caught too little and over-refused benign twins, and putting a slice of the parent's weights back into NORA broke the arm.

## Can one adapter carry both the behaviour and the condition?

Four ideas went into one round of training on a single audited data set of <Fact id="train_rows" /> rows (<Fact id="gen_cmds" /> generated commands, each on a start frame and a mid-episode frame, plus replay rows of the robot's own actions). Two reviewers checked it for leaks before training, and I rebuilt it until an automatic audit passed.

Three of the ideas mattered.

**A verdict token.** Instead of mapping a harmful command straight to a hold, the policy writes `[STOP]` or `[GO]` as text before its action tokens, on every policy call. The switch is exact, and because the verdict is decided again on every chunk, the robot can stop mid-task. On its own it did not generalize: trained on the labels I gave each command when I built it, the verdict caught <Fact id="verdict_construction_nt" /> of never-trained categories. With the parent's own text-only yes/no judgement as the label, <Fact id="verdict_parent_nt" />.

**Representation matching.** During training, an extra loss pulls the robot's representation of each command at the instruction words toward the parent's representation of the same command, stored once from <Fact id="match_layers_n" /> layers of the frozen parent. The matching term fell from <Fact id="align_loss_first" /> to <Fact id="align_loss_last" /> over training. I matched at the instruction words because that is where the diagnosis found the robot still carrying the parent's harm direction.

**A calibrated offset.** The matched verdict ranked harmful commands above benign ones, but left alone it said `[GO]` too often. A fixed bonus added to the `[STOP]` score sets how cautious it is. A <Fact id="calib_old_pct" /> rule over-refused <Fact id="calib_old_fa_min" /> to <Fact id="calib_old_fa_max" /> of hard benign pools at the first call, so I moved to a <Fact id="calib_pct" /> rule on <Fact id="calib_cmds" /> benign calibration commands that appear nowhere in training, and fixed it before any verdict install ran in the simulator.

Matching made the difference. With parent labels and no matching, the calibrated verdict caught <Fact id="unmatched_b95_nt" /> of never-trained categories at the first call. With matching, <Fact id="matched_b95_nt" />. The combination I call the parent-taught verdict passed every pre-registered rule on the selection episodes, refusing <Fact id="sel_nt_s0" /> of never-trained harm with task success <Fact id="sel_lib_s0" />, and a second seed refused <Fact id="sel_nt_s2" /> at <Fact id="sel_lib_s2" />. That matched the two-model gate with nothing resident beyond NORA and <Fact id="tokens_per_call" /> extra tokens per call.

## What this does and doesn't show

The claim is command-level refusal. Every harmful command I wrote, except <Fact id="twins_scene" /> twin, states its harm in words, and the verdict learned from text-only labels and from targets averaged over the instruction words. Harm that only the scene reveals mostly passes it. The paper's parent-gated reference shows that asking the parent at run time reaches the same refusal rates; the contribution is doing it with one model and no judge.

## Limitations

- One backbone, one policy family, in simulation, on <Fact id="libero_tasks" /> LIBERO-Goal tasks.
- Alarming benign wording trips it: at the calibrated offset every passing install holds on <Fact id="lex_min" /> to <Fact id="lex_max" /> of benign commands with words like "kill" or "smash" at the first call. A blind benign set written by an independent agent draws <Fact id="blind_min" /> to <Fact id="blind_max" /> holds against a target of <Fact id="blind_target" />.
- Small sets: <Fact id="nt_prompts" /> never-trained prompts and <Fact id="twins_n" /> twin prompts of each kind; <Fact id="lib_n" /> episodes behind each task-success value; three seeds of the main recipe and one of each ablation.
- No run matched to a non-parent teacher, so I cannot separate the parent's harm knowledge from a generic matching regulariser. The matching ablation rests on first-call holds, since the unmatched arm never ran in the simulator.
- Holding while carrying an object was trained but never exercised; swaps happen before any grasp. No jailbreak or adversarial commands were tested.
